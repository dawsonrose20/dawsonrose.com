Muscle to Music

An EMG instrument that lets students with limited movement below the neck play along in music class, using their jaw and neck muscles to make notes.

01

The challenge

Every student should be able to make music, including those who can't hold an instrument.

The William E. Carter School is a Boston public school for students aged 3 to 22 with intensive special needs and complex medical needs. Its music therapy program includes every student, but students with limited limb movement can't get the most out of hand-held instruments.

Our client wanted a real instrument that plays actual notes, not a game. Some students can only move their jaw or head, so the sensors had to work there. Because of sensory sensitivities, the electrodes also had to be comfortable to wear, ideally non-stick. We visited the class in person to see how students take part before we settled on a design.

02

How it works

When a muscle contracts, it gives off a tiny electrical signal called electromyography (EMG). The instrument picks up that signal on the jaw or neck, cleans it up, and turns each contraction into a musical note in real time.

  1. MuscleJaw or neck contracts
  2. ElectrodesBar or sticky pads on the skin
  3. Myoware 2.0Amplifies and filters the EMG
  4. Link shield3.5 mm cable to the base
  5. Arduino UnoReads and processes the signal
  6. Laptop appPlays the notes
Part 1

The hardware

An Arduino Uno with a Myoware Arduino Shield, in a 3D-printed case. It collects the EMG signal and sends it to the laptop over USB.

Part 2

The human connection

Custom electrode leads run from the student to the Myoware muscle sensor. They work with both bar electrodes and reusable sticky electrodes.

Part 3

The software

A laptop app built with a Google app builder. It takes the processed signal from a basic graph to a playable musical instrument.

04

Build it yourself

Part 1

The hardware

  • Arduino Uno R3
  • Myoware Arduino Shield
  • USB A to B cable
  • 2 × 3.5 mm male-to-male cables
  • 3D-printed case and foam pads

This part collects the EMG signal and sends it to the laptop. It's mostly simple assembly, but take care to seat the Myoware shield on the Arduino correctly.

  1. Attach the Myoware Arduino Shield to the Arduino board.
  2. Plug the two 3.5 mm cables into two different ports on the Myoware Link Shield.
  3. 3D print the housing.
  4. Fit the Arduino assembly into the printed housing.
  5. Route the cable out through the port.
Part 2

The human connection

  • Myoware Muscle Sensor 2.0
  • Myoware Link Shield
  • Bar and sticky electrodes
  • Wire and heat shrink
  • Nylon braided wire cover

This part carries the signal from the student to the Myoware sensor, and from there into the Arduino for processing. You'll need soldering equipment and a heat gun.

  1. Cut off the white snap connectors that come with the reusable sticky electrodes. These become an adapter that works with both bar electrodes and sticky electrodes.
  2. Solder about 3–4 ft of wire to each cut-off snap connector to lengthen the leads, which attach to the Myoware muscle sensor.
    • Use a different color for one wire so it's easy to tell it's the reference (ground).
    • Cover each soldered joint with heat shrink.
  3. Feed all three wires through the nylon braided cover.
Part 3

The software

  • Laptop

The app turns the processed signal into music. At first it only showed the EMG as a live graph. The final version plays real notes, so students can play along with songs.
